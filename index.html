About

Newtonian Gravity

The core of the simulation integrates Newton's law of universal gravitation across an N-body system. To prevent infinite forces and numerical instability during close encounters (singularities), a softening parameter $\epsilon$ is introduced.

The acceleration $\mathbf{a}_i$ of particle $i$ due to all other particles $j$ is calculated as:

$$ \mathbf{a}_i = G \sum_{j \neq i} \frac{m_j}{(\|\mathbf{r}_{ij}\|^2 + \epsilon^2)^{3/2}} \mathbf{r}_{ij} $$

Where $\mathbf{r}_{ij} = \mathbf{r}_j - \mathbf{r}_i$ is the distance vector, $m_j$ is the mass of the interacting particle, and $G$ is the gravitational constant.

Isothermal Dark Matter Halo

Visible matter alone cannot account for the observed flat rotation curves of galaxies. In the Galaxy (spiral) preset, the simulation models this without the computational overhead of millions of unseen particles by implementing an implicit Isothermal Dark Matter Halo force. (The halo is tunable and off by default in the Accretion Disk preset, where a single dominant black hole governs the dynamics.)

This adds an inward acceleration component directed towards the system's center, parameterized by:

$$ a_{dm} = -\frac{V_{flat}^2 \cdot r}{r^2 + \epsilon_{core}^2} $$

Where $V_{flat}$ represents the asymptotic circular velocity governed by the dark matter strength, and $\epsilon_{core}$ is the core radius of the halo.

Salpeter Initial Mass Function (IMF)

The mass distribution of stars in the simulation follows the realistic Salpeter Initial Mass Function, rather than a uniform or arbitrary assignment. The IMF describes the empirical distribution of initial masses for a population of stars.

$$ \xi(M) = \xi_0 M^{-2.35} $$

This power-law distribution means low-mass stars are exponentially more common than massive stars. In the accretion preset the sampled masses are sorted so the heaviest stars fill the active set that exerts gravity; in the galaxy preset the Salpeter draw sets only the star's colour, and every disk particle carries the same dynamical mass.

Stellar Color & The H-R Diagram

Star colors in the simulation are not randomly assigned; they are correlated with a star's mass, mapped roughly to the main sequence of the Hertzsprung-Russell (H-R) diagram.

More massive stars burn hotter and brighter, shifting their emission spectra towards the blue end, while less massive stars are cooler and emit primarily in the red spectrum. The simulation visually distinguishes stars by rendering the most massive stars with intense blue-white hues, grading down to yellow, orange, and deep red for the numerous lightweight stars.

Simulation Presets

The same N-body engine drives two distinct astrophysical regimes, selectable from the simulation's control panel:

  • Galaxy (spiral): A massive, self-gravitating exponential disk tuned to the Toomre $Q$ stability criterion and embedded in a dark-matter halo, which develops transient spiral arms as the stars shear and self-organise.
  • Accretion Disk (SMBH): A dominant central black hole surrounded by a collisionless, Keplerian test-particle disk - the ballistic limit, with no viscous inspiral. An adaptive timestep resolves the deep central well, and the dark-matter halo is off by default so the SMBH alone governs the orbits.

Symplectic Leapfrog Integration

Standard explicit Euler integration diverges rapidly for orbiting bodies, violating the conservation of energy. To solve this, the simulation utilizes a 2nd-order Symplectic Leapfrog Integrator. By staggering the velocity and position updates by a half-step $\Delta t / 2$, this method maintains long-term phase-space area and ensures orbital stability over thousands of revolutions.

$$ \mathbf{v}_{i}(t + \frac{\Delta t}{2}) = \mathbf{v}_{i}(t - \frac{\Delta t}{2}) + \mathbf{a}_{i}(t) \Delta t $$ $$ \mathbf{r}_{i}(t + \Delta t) = \mathbf{r}_{i}(t) + \mathbf{v}_{i}(t + \frac{\Delta t}{2}) \Delta t $$

Leapfrog integration guarantees time-reversibility and reliable energy conservation over long durations, making it a critical algorithm for high-fidelity N-body dynamics where performance and accuracy must be strictly balanced.

Brute Force Gravity (O(N²))

The most accurate, yet computationally expensive, method for calculating gravitational forces is the Direct N-Body (Brute Force) approach. In this model, every particle calculates its gravitational interaction with every other particle in the system.

While this makes the force summation exact - leaving the integrator and the softening length as the only sources of error - the time complexity scales quadratically at $O(N^2)$. Simulating 10,000 particles requires 100,000,000 force calculations per frame, making it intractable for large-scale N-body simulations on typical hardware, but useful as a baseline truth for smaller clusters.

Barnes-Hut & QuadTree (O(N log N))

To overcome quadratic scaling, the simulation implements the Barnes-Hut algorithm. This spatial partitioning method divides the 2D simulation space into a hierarchical QuadTree. Particles are recursively grouped into hierarchical nodes (cells).

If a group of distant particles (a node) is sufficiently far away from the particle being evaluated, the algorithm approximates the entire group as a single center of mass rather than calculating individual forces. This drastically reduces the time complexity to $O(N \log N)$, enabling the real-time simulation of significantly larger particle counts without a noticeable loss in macroscopic accuracy.

Active/Passive Computational Subsetting

Even with Barnes-Hut, rendering a visually dense N-body system of tens of thousands of stars (up to 200,000 on the GPU engine) on CPU threads is challenging. To solve this, we introduce an Active/Passive heuristic, splitting the bodies into two index ranges chosen by the preset:

  • Active set: the first activeCount bodies. Only these are inserted into the QuadTree (or summed on the GPU) and exert gravity. The accretion preset fills this range with the most massive stars; the galaxy preset with a random subsample of the disk that carries its full mass.
  • Passive set: every body after the active range. They exert no gravity and are not tree sources, but they feel the active set, the halo and the black hole exactly as active bodies do.

This hybrid approach effectively reduces the computational complexity from $O(N \log N)$ to $O(N \cdot k)$ where $k \ll N$, allowing for massive visual density while preserving accurate macroscopic N-body dynamics.

CPU Physics Engines & Threading

The simulation provides three selectable CPU-bound engines, each optimized for different classical N-body algorithms: a direct-sum Brute Force engine, a Barnes-Hut engine, and an off-thread Worker engine that runs Barnes-Hut in a dedicated Web Worker.

  • Brute Force Engine: Implements an $O(N^2)$ direct-sum calculation using kick-drift leapfrog integration (symplectic, with velocities staggered half a step ahead of positions). It deliberately computes each $i$-$j$ pair twice rather than exploiting Newton's Third Law, so that Brute Force and Barnes-Hut share a single force kernel (pairwiseAccel) - trading a 2× arithmetic cost for one identical, testable force law across engines.
  • Barnes-Hut Engine: Utilizes a QuadTree data structure to reduce the computational complexity to $O(N \log N)$. This engine dynamically reconstructs the spatial tree every frame and evaluates forces based on a distance threshold $\theta$.

Brute Force and Barnes-Hut step the simulation on the main thread. The Worker engine instead moves Barnes-Hut off it: a WorkerBridge spawns a dedicated Web Worker (physics.worker.ts), so a slow step costs frame rate rather than interface responsiveness. Memory is not copied between threads; instead, a SharedArrayBuffer is utilized to provide zero-copy concurrent memory access. The main thread and the worker synchronize through a single atomic status flag using Atomics.wait and Atomics.notify, with no locking overhead.

The handshake is deliberately lock-free rather than strict. If the worker is still mid-step when the next frame arrives, the request is dropped instead of queued, and simulated time advances only by the number of steps the worker actually reports finishing - so the clock stays honest under load rather than accumulating a backlog. Renders are gated on the same flag: while a step is in flight the renderer holds the last completed frame, so the main thread never evaluates a half-drawn coordinate map.

GPU Physics & Compute Architecture

For maximum performance and particle density, the simulation delegates both physics integration and rendering to the Graphics Processing Unit via the WebGPU API.

  • Compute Shaders: Gravitational forces and velocity updates are calculated in parallel across thousands of GPU cores using WGSL compute shaders (shaders.wgsl). The compute pipeline employs a "ping-pong" buffer architecture: in frame $A$, it reads state from Buffer 1 and writes the updated state to Buffer 2. In frame $B$, it reverses the process. This guarantees that all instances read from a consistent state without complex thread synchronization.
  • Hardware Optimizations: The gravity kernel leverages specialized hardware instructions, such as inverseSqrt, to compute the inverse distance required for the gravitational force vector as a single intrinsic rather than a division and a square root.
  • Unified Render Pipeline: Unlike typical implementations, the calculated particle positions and velocities are never read back to the CPU memory (preventing costly PCI-e bus transfers). Instead, the GPU's Render Pipeline directly reads the compute shader's output buffer (Storage Buffer) as Instance Data. Particles are drawn as heavily optimized instanced Quads, with perfect circular appearances achieved via fragment shader pixel discarding and mathematical gradients.
